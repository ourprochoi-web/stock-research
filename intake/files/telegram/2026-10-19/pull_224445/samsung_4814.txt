Amazon's TorchNeuron runs existing PyTorch code on

TorchNeuron will be open-sourced at the PyTorch Conference on October 20.
Startups and enterprises in robotics, video, world models, self-driving cars, speech, drug discovery, LLMs, and finance are already building on it.
Today, 85% of AI researchers and developers write their code in PyTorch. Until now, the hardware that code could run on was far more limited than the code itself, and expanding to new chips meant rewriting and revalidating work that took months to perfect.
Amazon is now closing that gap. With
TorchNeuron
, the PyTorch code developers already write runs natively on Amazon's
AWS Trainium chips
, with a single configuration change and no rewrite. TorchNeuron will be released under an open-source license at the PyTorch Conference North America on October 20.

In a nondescript building in Cupertino, California, a team of engineers is quietly designing the networking technology that keeps the modern world connected—one wavelength of light at a time.
What does “no code change” actually mean?
Think of your AI code as a car you've spent years fine-tuning. The engine, the suspension—every part working together. A new, more affordable engine comes along with serious horsepower, but installing it would mean tearing down the car and rebuilding it piece by piece. TorchNeuron lets you swap in the new engine without taking the car apart.
In a side-by-side demonstration, Amazon engineers ran the same AI training script on a conventional chip and on
Trainium
. The only difference was a setting that told the code which chip to use. Results showed that the AI model learned the same things at the same rate.
For developers, this is significant. PyTorch already has a built-in way for new hardware to register itself. TorchNeuron plugs into that standard mechanism, meaning the tools developers already use to test, debug, and scale their AI models all work on Trainium without modification.

Why TorchNeuron matters for developers and for the industry
The real cost of
AI
goes beyond the price of a chip. For many companies, especially
startups
, the most expensive line item is engineering time. Every week a developer spends adapting software for new hardware is a week not spent improving the product. For small teams with limited resources, that trade-off can contribute to whether a company succeeds or fails.
And it’s not just startups. For large enterprises running massive AI fleets, the ability to use the same hardware for both
training and inference
is a game changer. During off-peak hours, capacity that served inference workloads can shift seamlessly to training jobs, getting more value from the same hardware instead of leaving specialized hardware idle.
By reducing the friction of adopting new hardware, TorchNeuron introduces something the AI industry has largely lacked: meaningful choice. When developers can move between chips without penalty, chipmakers have to earn developers' business, and developers benefit from lower costs and faster iteration.
