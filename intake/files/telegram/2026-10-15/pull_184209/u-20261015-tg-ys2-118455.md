Intel's Advanced Packaging Roadmap Revealed: EMIB-T Mass Production Next Year, Targeting 12x Reticle-Size AI Chips
Intel is transforming advanced packaging technology from a capability reserved for high-end processors into a core weapon for capturing the AI chip market. According to the company's latest disclosed roadmap, its Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge (EMIB) and Foveros packaging platforms are simultaneously evolving toward finer interconnect pitches, larger package dimensions, and glass substrates, with the goal of achieving 12x reticle-size system-in-package (SiP) around 2027—directly challenging TSMC's CoWoS dominance in AI accelerator packaging.
Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan revealed during the August 2026 earnings call that customer interest in EMIB-T "remains very high," with order backlogs continuing to grow and yields and reliability meeting expectations. The company is focused on supporting customers' capacity ramp in 2027. Behind this statement lies nearly a decade of Intel's technical accumulation in advanced packaging, as well as a strategic window to capture overflow orders amid TSMC's CoWoS capacity shortages.
EMIB: From Silicon Bridge to Vertical Power Delivery
The core concept of EMIB technology has remained largely unchanged since its first mass production in 2017: rather than using expensive large silicon interposers, micro silicon bridges are embedded into organic package substrates, deployed only where high-density die-to-die connections are needed. This approach dramatically reduces silicon material usage while allowing the rest of the package to use conventional substrate routing and looser bump pitches.
Early EMIB used a 55-micron first-level interconnect (FLI) bump pitch, later reduced to 45 microns. According to the latest roadmap, this pitch will shrink further to approximately 36 microns, ultimately reaching 25 microns, significantly increasing the number of connections that can be accommodated at the chip edge. But pitch reduction is only one dimension of the evolution. Intel is simultaneously adding more functionality to the bridge itself: EMIB-M integrates metal-insulator-metal (MIM) capacitors within the bridge to improve power delivery and suppress voltage fluctuations; the newer EMIB-T uses through-silicon vias (TSVs) to enable power to pass vertically through the bridge, avoiding longer routing paths—particularly critical for multi-kilowatt SiPs and HBM4-class memory subsystems.
The leap in package size is equally striking. Intel has progressed from 4x reticle-size SiPs on 80×80mm packages in the early 2000s to nearly 8x reticle size on current 120×120mm packages, with plans to achieve 12x reticle size on 120×180mm packages in the future. The final form will combine finer-pitch EMIB with glass-core substrates—glass offers better stability and lower warpage compared to organic materials, an advantage that becomes increasingly pronounced as package sizes continue to grow.
The Foveros Family: Three Variants Positioned Against CoWoS
Since its debut with the Lakefield processor in 2019, Foveros packaging technology has expanded from an initial chip-on-wafer solution into a complete product family covering 2.5D and 3D integration. The latest 2.5D roadmap released in mid-2025 defines three variants: Foveros-S, Foveros-R, and Foveros-B, corresponding respectively to TSMC's CoWoS-S, CoWoS-R, and CoWoS-L.
Foveros-S is the most advanced and production-proven solution, placing multiple chiplets on a silicon interposer. It currently supports packages up to 4x reticle size, below TSMC's CoWoS-S at 5.5x reticle. Foveros-R completely eliminates the silicon interposer, adopting fan-out redistribution layer (RDL) technology with up to three RDL layers, striking a balance between cost and interconnect density, with mass production planned for 2027. Foveros-B combines RDL with localized active or passive silicon bridges, providing high-density connections only where needed, positioned between pure RDL solutions and large silicon interposers.
All three variants support sub-25-micron pitch targets, with bump density expanding from 772 connections per square millimeter to 1,600, and die-to-die interconnect power consumption of 0.15 picojoules per bit. Notably, Intel has not explicitly stated that Foveros-B uses EMIB bridges, but given the similar microbump pitches and plans for active local bridges in the future, industry observers widely believe its bridges are highly similar to EMIB-M and EMIB-T.
Foveros Direct 3D: Copper-to-Copper Hybrid Bonding
The most architecturally significant innovation in the Foveros roadmap is Foveros Direct 3D, which completely eliminates solder in favor of direct copper-to-copper hybrid bonding. Traditional microbumps cannot shrink indefinitely because solder bumps require sufficient size to ensure manufacturing yield and reliability, and solder itself introduces additional resistance and parasitic effects. Foveros Direct 3D bonds copper pads on one die directly to copper pads on another, achieving interconnect pitches below 10 microns—10 times finer than traditional microbumps.
Intel claims the technology can achieve interconnect density of up to 10,000 connections per square millimeter, with die-to-die interconnect power consumption below 0.05 picojoules per bit. Higher-density connections shorten signal paths between stacked dies, increasing bandwidth, reducing latency, and cutting data transfer energy consumption. Direct bonding also creates a more continuous thermal path, improving heat dissipation—critical for scenarios where vertically stacked logic circuits significantly increase power density.
The first product to use Foveros Direct 3D copper bonding in mass production is the Xeon 6+ "Clearwater Forest" processor, which connects Intel 18A compute dies to an active base die using first-generation 9-micron copper bonding pitch. Intel plans to shrink the pitch to 3 microns in the second generation. For reference, TSMC's first-generation SoIC launched in 2023 supports 9-micron pitch, while the latest SoIC generation in mass production in 2025 is at 6 microns, with expectations to shrink to 4.5 microns by 2029.
EMIB 3.5D: Scaling Horizontally and Vertically Simultaneously
Foveros and EMIB need not operate independently. Intel can combine Foveros 2.5D or Foveros Direct 3D with substrate-embedded EMIB bridges to form what is called EMIB 3.5D architecture. In this architecture, Foveros provides high-density local connections within individual chip components, while EMIB delivers high-bandwidth horizontal interconnect between modules—essentially building a large system-in-package from smaller SiPs.
Intel first used EMIB 3.5D to build the Ponte Vecchio compute GPU, integrating 47 active tiles manufactured across five process nodes, containing over 100 billion transistors. While the Data Center GPU Max was not a commercial success, it demonstrated Intel's engineering prowess in advanced packaging.
However, Intel decided not to use EMIB 3.5D in the next-generation Xeon "Diamond Rapids" processor: while that CPU employs Foveros Direct 3D for vertical integration, it opted for UCIe-S connections for horizontal interconnect. This choice suggests that even within Intel, full deployment of EMIB 3.5D depends on product-specific performance, cost, and power trade-offs.
Market Competition and Supply Chain Challenges
TSMC's CoWoS has become the mainstream packaging solution for AI servers thanks to its high bandwidth and low latency, but demand has clearly outstripped supply. Analysts estimate that after TSMC's capacity expansion, CoWoS monthly capacity could reach 185,000 to 190,000 wafers, with the supply-demand gap potentially narrowing from approximately 30%–35% to 15%–20%, but overflow demand must still be absorbed by OSAT providers. Some orders have already flowed to United Microelectronics, Amkor, and other players.
After expanding EMIB/Foveros capacity in Malaysia and with outsourcing partners, Intel has reported that 18A process yields have climbed to 80%, indicating that advanced packaging mass production stability is improving. However, industry sources note that EMIB-T and Foveros still lag CoWoS in absolute bandwidth and latency performance, and whether they can fully substitute in more critical applications remains to be seen.
Materials and equipment also pose bottlenecks. High-end ABF substrates, low-dielectric-constant glass fiber cloth, glass substrate TGV, and other critical materials and processes remain tight, with some industry players estimating that approximately 10%–15% of potential revenue cannot be fulfilled in the short term due to material shortages. Mitsubishi Chemical has announced it is applying computational chemistry and quantum technologies to accelerate development of thermal interface materials, warpage control, and high-purity cleaning chemicals, attempting to shorten trial-and-error and ramp-up cycles from the materials side.
In Intel's current packaging portfolio, wafer-scale system platforms remain a blank spot—wafer-scale processors similar to Cerebras WSE and Tesla Dojo have not yet appeared in its roadmap. Given that market demand for such technologies is currently extremely limited and unlikely to grow significantly in the near term, this trade-off is commercially understandable.
From a broader perspective, the focus of advanced packaging competition is shifting from pure capacity competition to who can more quickly bring yields, material supply, and costs onto the mass production track simultaneously. Intel is leveraging the complementary architecture of EMIB and Foveros to carve out a significant market share from TSMC in this round of competition.
