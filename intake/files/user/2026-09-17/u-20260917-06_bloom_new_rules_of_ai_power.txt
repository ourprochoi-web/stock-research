The modern power system was born at the end of the nineteenth century as cities were 
growing, factories were expanding, and electricity began to transform how people lived and 
worked. The new economy needed electricity for lighting, streetcars, elevators, motors, and 
industrial machinery. It also needed a practical way to generate electricity at scale and deliver 
it over long distances to where it was needed.
Alternating current (AC) made that possible. Its voltage could be raised for efficient 
transmission over long distances and then lowered again near homes, businesses, and 
factories to levels that were safe and practical to use. That advantage helped establish 
a model built around large, centralized power plants connected to customers through 
transmission and distribution lines. Over the 20th century, that architecture expanded into the 
grid we know today. It was extraordinarily effective for the mechanical age it was built to serve.
Today’s digital age looks very different. The machines that relied on motors and mechanical 
systems are increasingly enabled by computers, artificial intelligence, and software. Factories 
run on automation and robotics. Hospitals depend on advanced imaging, monitoring, and 
diagnostic systems. Everyday life depends on smartphones and connected devices. Offices, 
communications networks, and data centers rely on computing at enormous scale. At the 
heart are semiconductor-based electronics powered by direct current (DC). Yet most of the 
electricity that powers the digital economy is still delivered as AC.
Until recently, that mismatch was manageable. Data centers brought AC power into the 
facility and converted it to DC at the rack level. At conventional rack densities, the amount of 
equipment, copper, and space required remained relatively modest. AI changes the scale. Rack 
power is expected to reach 600 kilowatts (kW) by 2027, with a path toward 1 megawatt (MW).1 
At those densities, converting and moving power becomes far more costly and complex.
That leaves data center developers with a consequential choice: continue with the existing 
AC power model and convert it to DC, or generate DC power onsite and avoid much of the 
conversion altogether. The decision reaches beyond electrical design, affecting equipment, 
construction timelines, reliability, and available space.
The financial stakes are enormous. In 2026, the five largest U.S. hyperscalers are expected 
to deploy about $750 billion in capital.2 At that scale, even relatively small differences in 
infrastructure cost translate into billions of dollars.
This report compares the leading approaches to delivering DC power at AI scale and the 
choices they create for developers. The path they choose will help determine whether power 
infrastructure enables the next generation of AI compute or becomes a constraint.

Rapidly growing compute density is driving a shift in data center electrical 
architecture. AI compute is outrunning the power infrastructure built to support it. 
Power demand per rack has increased roughly twenty-fold since 2020 and is expected 
to reach 600 kW by 2027, with a path toward 1 MW.3 At those densities, scaling today’s 
AC power architecture is more cumbersome and expensive: impractical amounts 
of copper, additional electrical equipment, and valuable space devoted to power 
infrastructure rather than compute. The industry’s response is 800V DC, which can 
deliver far more power with less current and is emerging as the standard for next-
generation AI systems. NVIDIA will require 800V DC beginning with its Rubin Ultra and 
Kyber rack architecture. For data center developers, the question now is how 800V 
DC power will be supplied.
The default assumption is to preserve existing AC power sources and convert 
their output to DC. The grid and most sources of data center power still deliver AC 
power, even as next-generation AI systems move to DC. The industry bridges the gap 
to bring AC power into the data center and convert it to 800V DC. That still requires 
substantial electrical infrastructure with year-long lead times. Some designs also 
depend on technologies with less operating history, such as solid-state transformers 
that add to supply-chain and operational risk. The result is a new power architecture 
built on old infrastructure. 
There is another path: generate DC power natively and avoid costly and complex 
AC-to-DC conversion. If AI racks require DC power, the most direct path is to 
generate it from the beginning. This eliminates much of the equipment required 
to convert AC power and reduces the number of components to buy, install, and 
maintain. It also reduces dependence on long-lead electrical equipment and potential 
points of failure. With fewer conversion stages, less power is lost along the way and 
less space is consumed by electrical infrastructure. The result is a more streamlined 
and less costly path from generation to consumption.
Fuel cells are the only DC-native source that can provide continuous power at 
gigawatt scale. Very few onsite power sources can meet all three requirements of an 
800V DC data center: generate DC natively, run continuously, and scale to gigawatt 
loads. Solar generates DC but cannot provide continuous power. Batteries operate on 
DC but store electricity rather than generate it. Turbines and reciprocating engines 
can provide continuous power at scale, but they generate AC and retain much of the 
same conversion infrastructure as a grid-powered site. Fuel cells are the exception: 
they generate DC natively and can run continuously. Bloom Energy is the only fuel 
cell provider that operates at scale for large AI data centers and combines all three 
requirements into one onsite power source.

Delivering that much power in lower-voltage power architecture causes electrical losses and 
requires significantly more equipment, copper, and space that could be used for compute. In 
addition, the required 400 pounds of copper for each rack becomes impractical.5
Therefore, the industry is converging on 800V DC as the power architecture for next-
generation AI systems. Higher voltage allows the same amount of power to be delivered 
at lower current and requires significantly less copper and equipment. For data center 
developers, this shift is a necessity rather than a choice. 
AI-native companies with these rack architectures will feel the impact first. Beginning with 
Rubin Ultra and Kyber rack architecture, NVIDIA has specified 800V DC for 2027 and all 
generations after it. Facilities not designed for the new architecture will require substantial 
changes. Developers planning for that generation of chips must build for 800V DC whether or 
not they have chosen the standard. 
The transition is already underway. An August 2026 Bernstein survey of 50 North American 
data center procurement leaders found that 15% already have an 800V DC project under 
construction or in operation, and another 29% expect to begin one by 2027.6 Bloom Energy’s 
2026 Mid-Year Pulse Data Center Power Report found a similar trend where data center 
leaders expect DC-based architectures to account for 58% of new deployments by 2030.7
The question now is how 800V DC power will be supplied. Power architecture is determined 
years before a facility opens and is expensive to revise after the fact. With 800V DC adoption 
expected to accelerate in 2028 and 2029, developers targeting that window need to be 
making those decisions now.

The grid and most power sources provide AC power, even as next-generation AI systems move 
to DC. The prevailing response is to preserve that model: source AC power from the grid and 
convert it to 800V DC. Even when racks move to 800V DC, a grid-powered site still needs 
a substation, medium-voltage switchgear, AC-to-DC conversion equipment, and backup 
power to carry the load through an outage. The equipment is costly and leaves developers 
exposed to year-long lead times on large transformers and switchgear (Exhibit 2). As a result, 
developers incur expense and schedule risk associated with conventional AC architecture.

The industry’s answer to multiple transformation and conversion steps is a solid-state 
transformer that collapses medium voltage transformation and AC-to-DC conversion into 
one system and replaces several pieces of conventional electrical equipment. But solid-state 
transformers are newer technology with less operating history. No suppliers are producing 
solid-state transformers at the volumes a gigawatt data center needs, and service practices and 
operating history are thinner than those for conventional transformers. Developers must choose 
between mature equipment that carries the full cost and lead-time burden, or new equipment 
that does not yet exist at scale. The underlying constraint remains the same: power arrives as AC.

Starting with DC eliminates much of the equipment required to convert AC power (Exhibit 3). 
Power produced as DC needs no conversion, simplifying the path from the generation source 
to the rack. There are fewer components to purchase, install, operate, and maintain. It’s a more 
reliable system with fewer points of failure and less electrical maintenance across the life of 
the facility.8 Fewer conversion stages also reduce electrical losses and free up space inside the 
data center for compute.

that DC capacity can be added as demand grows without reconfiguring equipment.

When power is generated as DC from the outset, data center sites do not need the conversion 
equipment described in Section 2. Equipment in an AC-powered conventional build-out is 
costly; eliminating it significantly lowers capital costs. The challenge is finding an onsite power 
source that combines three requirements: native DC generation, continuous output, and the 
ability to operate at data center scale. Most available technologies meet only some of these 
requirements (Exhibit 4).
Solar generates DC power natively, but its output is intermittent and cannot independently 
support a continuous gigawatt-scale load. Batteries also operate on DC, but they store rather than 
generate electricity, which limits their role as a primary source of power. Turbines and reciprocating 
engines can provide continuous power at scale, but they generate AC; therefore, an 800V DC 
data center powered by those technologies still requires the transformation and conversion 
infrastructure and encounters the long lead times for equipment described in Section 2. 
Starting with DC also leaves the distribution architecture open. Bloom’s fuel cells deliver 800V DC 
with a center point, allowing customers to use either a monopolar or ±400V DC bipolar 
architecture from one platform. Fuel cells are the only DC-native generation technology 
capable of delivering continuous DC power at AI scale and Bloom is the only fuel cell provider 
currently delivering that capability at scale for AI data centers.

architecture is an exception and is the reason two facilities of identical capacity and running 
identical hardware can differ by billions of dollars in what they cost to build and operate.

Conclusion
The chip roadmap is increasingly setting the 
terms for data center design. The racks arriving 
in 2027 assume a power architecture that most 
facilities do not yet have. How developers supply 
that power will determine whether they can deploy 
each new generation as it arrives or retrofit their 
infrastructure to catch up. Starting with DC frees 
more capital for the part of the data center that 
creates the most value: compute.
