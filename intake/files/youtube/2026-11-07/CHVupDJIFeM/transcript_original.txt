Mounjaro has continued its sensation in Korea, nearing 1 trillion won in cumulative sales just one year after its launch. In the second quarter of this year, it even took the top spot for prescription drug distribution in Korea . However, despite this immense popularity , complaints that the domestic retail price is too expensive are growing louder. They say it’s a quarter of the price in Japan, so why is it so expensive only in our country? Mounjaro is a GLP-1 receptor agonist obesity treatment. It mimics the satiety hormone GLP-1, reducing appetite and maintaining fullness to help people lose weight naturally. Known for its significant weight loss effects, it is receiving a lot of attention in Korea in particular. But when you look at the price of Mounjaro ...The price for a one-month supply of Mounjaro in Korea is about 280,000 to 350,000 won. On the other hand, in Japan , it is around 10,000 yen, or about 85,000 won in our currency. The price difference between Korea and Japan reaches up to four times. Coupled with the weak yen, it has become more cost-effective to get a Mounjaro prescription in Japan. This has led to the phenomenon of people bringing Mounjaro from Japan, dubbed " Sushi-jaro." However, even if a drug is officially prescribed overseas, you cannot bring it into Korea without meeting import requirements. Despite this, according to the Korea Customs Service, the number of intercepted illegal imports of obesity drugs like Mounjaro and Wegovy surged from 4 in 2024 to 5,030 as of August this year. The price gap between domestic and international markets is fueling an increase in illegal import attempts. Why is there such a massive price difference for the same drug between Korea and Japan? >> Eli Lilly likely employs different pricing strategies for each market. It’s probably due to the differences in supply prices. Second, in Japan, it is covered by insurance, albeit limited to diabetes, while in Korea it is not covered, which is likely the fundamental difference. >> In Japan, when Mounjaro is used for diabetes treatment, it is covered by health insurance, so it follows the government-set fair price rather than the pharmaceutical company's price. Once listed as a covered drug, the government can further lower the price based on certain requirements. In fact, Japan applied a pricing system to Mounjaro due to its growing sales volume and cut its official price by 25 %last August. >> If sales volume is high, the drug price is lowered. Since it reached number one in sales in Japan, the price for all dosages was reduced by 25%in August. In our country, Mounjaro remains entirely non-reimbursable, regardless of indications or co-existing conditions. The government limits its price-volume linkage system to drugs covered by insurance. So, pharmaceutical companies set their own supply prices, and then markups are added as the drugs pass through wholesalers, pharmacies, and hospitals. That is why, even though over 1 trillion won worth was sold, the price remains almost the same. >> On the other hand, in Korea, Mounjaro is a non-reimbursable drug, so the government does not manage its price. The National Health Insurance Service does not negotiate drug prices separately with pharmaceutical companies either. Since it is outside the reimbursement system, price control itself is completely impossible in this situation. Ultimately, it is a structure where the price burden inevitably falls back on the consumer. In response, Eli Lilly Korea, which sells Mounjaro, and Novo Nordisk, the maker of Wegovy, have pushed for health insurance coverage. Eli Lilly Korea reapplied this past June after negotiations over drug prices fell through following their first application. Novo Nordisk also applied for Wegovy's coverage last October, and the review process is currently underway . However, applying health insurance to obesity treatments is not a simple matter. While coverage reduces the burden on patients, it also increases the costs that the health insurance fund must bear. >> Reimbursement means that health insurance pays for most of the drug's cost. It means the consumer only pays a very small portion. But considering where health insurance funding comes from, it acts like a tax that subsidizes a large part of the costs, so if the drug is used extensively, it will create a burden on the health insurance fund. >> So, patients who were hesitant to seek treatment due to cost or concerns about side effects could find it easier to get the drug prescribed, which may lead to issues with abuse or misuse. As health insurance funds are limited, there is also concern that if finances are injected into Mounjaro, funds available for expanding coverage for other drugs or treatments could decrease. The issue of health insurance coverage was also discussed during the recent parliamentary audit. Rep. Nam In-soon argued that with the growing problem of misuse of obesity drugs like Mounjaro, enforcement alone has limitations, so obesity treatment injections should be included for consideration under managed coverage. In response, Minister of Health and Welfare Cho Kyoo-hong stated that he would review the matter. Here, " managed coverage" refers to a system where the government sets prices and treatment standards to manage certain non-reimbursable items with high risks of overutilization within the health insurance system. >> Currently, because they are non-reimbursable, they aren't managed, and prices are being set arbitrarily. But introducing managed coverage means setting a price for the patient's out-of-pocket share. The purpose of this is to establish a benchmark for drug prices. >> So, even if it is difficult to apply general health insurance coverage, the government intends to control prices and manage prescription standards by incorporating them into managed coverage . However, since it is operated with government support along with taxpayer money, there are many factors to consider. >> High demand for the drug is just one of the reasons. There must be a great deal of other evidence as well. For instance, it is decided whether to cover a treatment or drug under insurance based on comprehensive factors , such as whether it treats a severe illness with high mortality if left untreated, or if there are absolutely no other alternatives. >> In any case, there seem to be quite a few hurdles to overcome before it can be included in the reimbursement system. Meanwhile, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety is pushing to designate foreign weight-loss drugs like Wegovy and Mounjaro as medications subject to potential misuse. However, some point out that simply designating them as such or cracking down on overseas imports is not enough to address the root problem caused by domestic and international price gaps. >> The policy reason is safety, but from what I see, relying solely on crackdowns clearly has its limits. Because the price difference is so large, how could we possibly regulate this? Therefore, what's important alongside enforcement is price rationalization. Prices should either be brought into the reimbursement system or lowered through competition; these approaches need to go hand in hand. >> Amidst this, a domestic weight-loss drug , Eppe, has entered the market with price competitiveness as its strength. The cost for the first month of treatment with Eppe is around 160,000 won. This is about 43%cheaper than Mounjaro, which costs around 280,000 won . If the arrival of Eppe triggers full-scale price competition, existing products might also move to adjust their prices. In fact, ahead of Mounjaro’s domestic launch last August, Wegovy also cut the supply price of its starter doses by up to 42%.
